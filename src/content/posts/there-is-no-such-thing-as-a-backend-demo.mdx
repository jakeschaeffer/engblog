We do a lot of eval-driven development. Almost every engagement we take on lives or dies on how
well we can measure a model's behaviour against the real world, and how quickly we can act on what
those measurements tell us. Because evals sit at the centre of our work, we've invested more than
most teams would in how we build and look at them — and that investment has taught us something
that applies well beyond evals.

Here's the thesis: building a custom UI is no longer the expensive, multi-week detour it used to
be. So much of frontend development today is AI-assisted that spinning up a purpose-built tool is
fast, cheap, and often cleaner than retrofitting a generic one. That changes the default answer to
"should we build our own tool for this?" from _no_ to _why not?_

## Why now: custom UIs are cheap to build

Internal tools used to cost weeks of frontend time that was almost always better spent elsewhere.
AI-assisted development collapsed that cost.

That shift matters more than it sounds like it should. When a custom tool is cheap to build, you're
no longer locked into a generic tool's opinions about layout, emphasis, or which metrics deserve
the spotlight. You can pull in whatever frontend stack your company already standardises on for
consistency, or reach for something newer and better suited to the problem — without worrying about
long-term lock-in either way, because the cost of building it again if you change your mind is low.

The net effect: "should we build a custom UI for this" stops being a resourcing question and
becomes a design decision. That's a different kind of conversation, and it opens the door to tools
that would never have gotten built otherwise.

## There is no such thing as a backend demo

No matter how much good engineering happened behind the scenes, a client can't see it without a
front end. You can have the cleanest pipeline, the most rigorous eval harness, and a model that's
genuinely improving — and none of it will land in a demo if the audience can't _see_ it. As far as
they're concerned, if it isn't visible, it didn't happen.

That's why demos live or die on visual communication, and why the bar for a demo tool is higher
than most people assume: it has to be clean, legible, and genuinely user-friendly, not just
functional. Very few generic, off-the-shelf tools clear that bar for a specific project's story —
they're built to be broadly useful, not to make _your_ work look as good as it actually is.

This is exactly where a custom UI earns its cost. It isn't built to hit a generic bar. It's built
to hit yours.

## Putting the right metrics front and centre

To be clear: you can dig up precision, recall, false positive rate, and per-category breakdowns in
plenty of off-the-shelf eval tools. That's not the differentiator, and we're not claiming
otherwise.

The differentiator is control over presentation. A custom UI lets you decide what's the headline
and what's secondary — tuned to what a specific client or engineering effort actually cares about —
instead of accepting whatever a generic dashboard decided was important.

On our own eval dashboard, here's what we chose to put front and centre, and why:

- **Precision, recall, and false positive rate** — macro-averaged across categories, because a
  single accuracy number flattens too much.
- **Latency** — end-to-end time per video, because speed is part of the product experience.
- **A projected `$/Sub/Mo` figure** — because turning a per-video cost into a monthly
  cost-per-subscriber number is what actually matters to the business, not the raw dollar figure.
- **Per-category breakdowns** — the same metrics above, scoped to a single classification category.

## Case study: the dot layer

Here's one concrete example of what that freedom bought us on a specific client engagement. (The
screenshots and demo below are a recreation of the same idea against a generic, made-up
classification problem — not the client's actual tool or data — since the original isn't ours to
share.) This isn't a prescription — we're not arguing every eval UI should look like this. It's an
illustration of what becomes possible when you build your own tool instead of adapting to someone
else's.

The problem: the third-party eval tool we started with had a real learning curve and wasn't
pleasant to look at, which made it a hard sell for daily use, let alone for a client demo.

So we built a different way to look at the data. Every video in an eval run becomes a single small
dot, grouped by predicted category and coloured by whether the prediction was correct. Instead of a
table you'd have to filter and sort, you get a field of dots where distribution, class imbalance,
and error clusters are visible at a glance — the pattern jumps out before you've asked a single
query.

Click any dot and it expands into the full picture for that one item: the actual video clip, the
model's caption, predicted label against ground truth, confidence score, objects detected, and that
item's specific latency and cost.

The payoff is the combination, not either piece alone. The aggregate metric tells you something's
wrong. The dot grid shows you where. The popup tells you why. All of it lives on one screen, with
no context switching between a metrics dashboard, a log viewer, and a video player. We only got to
design something this specific to our problem because we built our own tool instead of working
within a generic one's layout.

## Why it matters

For engineers, this collapses the triage loop. "Accuracy dropped two points" turns into "here are
the four clips actually responsible for it" in seconds instead of an afternoon, which keeps the
team's attention on the eval-driven priorities that matter instead of on guesswork.

For clients, the same tool doubles as a demo built around their exact story — hitting the bar the
"no backend demo" principle demands. Real clips, real numbers, and failures shown as openly as
wins, instead of a generic dashboard retrofitted to look like it was built for them.

## Takeaway

The question worth asking isn't "can an existing tool show me this metric." Most of the time, the
answer is yes. The question is: can we build something better — faster than we think, and built
exactly for how we need to communicate this work?

Custom UIs used to be expensive enough that the honest answer was usually no. AI-assisted
development changed that answer. Evals happen to be where we've felt that shift the most, because
eval-driven development is so central to how we work — but the underlying idea isn't specific to
evals at all. Custom UIs are cheap enough now to be the default, not the exception, for any tool
where seeing the work clearly is the whole point.
